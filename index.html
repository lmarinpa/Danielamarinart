Casa Cara Blanca

A series of abstract portraits of one family, created for the architecture and light of their home. Below is the process behind the project.

Client
Private family residence
Year
2026
Works
6 works, acrylic on canvas and sculptural relief on wood
With
Interior designer / architect
Role
Concept, proposals, renderings, painting, delivery

The works

IContext

The Brief

The family was redesigning their home with an interior designer and an architect, and invited me as the commissioned artist to create a private collection: one-of-a-kind works conceived for this house alone.

The goal was not decorative art that could hang anywhere, but pieces that represent the family and are designed for the scale, light and architecture of each space.

The project had three requirements: fit the architecture, represent the family, and give the clients confidence in the work before production began.

IIMy Role (Individual Contribution)

From Concept to Delivery

Working alongside the architect who led the project, I created the collection from the first sketch to its final installation. The vision was shaped in close collaboration with the family, the architect and the interior designer. My role was to translate it into finished works, realized with precision and exactly as envisioned.

  • Shaped the vision together with the family, interior designer and architect
  • Developed the concept connecting each piece to the family
  • Presented a range of proposals for the family to choose from
  • Created digital renderings of the selected works in the actual rooms
  • Created and installed every piece exactly as approved
IIIResearch Methods Used

IVKey Insights

What I Learned

  1. 01

    Presenting concrete options made it easier for the family to decide than describing preferences from scratch.

  2. 02

Read moreClose

  2. 04

VDesign Decisions (and Why)

Delivered as Approved

The approved proposals were delivered as presented. The main decisions, and why:

  • Four colors, four forms. Every piece is built from four elements, one per family member. This made each work personal and kept six different pieces reading as one series.
  • Sized to each wall. Dimensions came from the site measurements, so each piece fits its wall, its light and the way it is seen from the room.
  • Approved in renderings first. Showing each work in the actual room before painting removed the risk of a large commission the family could not preview.
  • Painting and relief together. White Portrait and River of Four Currents combine sculpted relief with paint, so the room's light changes how they read through the day.

VIOutcomes / Evaluation

Results

The family received works that matched the approved proposals, plus one additional piece developed at their request.

  • 6 works delivered: acrylic on canvas and sculptural relief on wood, for the living room, hallway and main bedroom.
  • Scale, color and placement matched the approved renderings, as the side-by-side comparisons above show.
  • Delivered with documentation: a story for each work and care instructions for the family.
VIIReflection & Next Steps

Next Time

With user-centered design methods, next time I would add:

  • A follow-up interview with the family a few months after installation
  • Observation of how each room is used before deciding size and placement
  • Earlier testing of two or three rendering options per wall

Beauty and the Beast

I led the paint team for Beauty and the Beast at Tokyo Disneyland, a production valued at more than two million dollars.

Client
Walt Disney Imagineering
Place
Tokyo Disneyland, Japan
Role
Scenic paint team lead
Scale
A production valued at more than $2 million
IContext

A story you walk into

In a theme-park attraction, the scenery is the story. Every painted surface has to match the approved design and look the same across a large site, painted by many hands.

IIMy role

Leading the paint team

I directed the scenic paint team for the attraction.

As a scenic art lead, my work is to:

  • Create and present paint samples to the client
  • Work with the client from sampling through installation
  • Turn approved samples into paint procedures that team members of all skill levels can follow
  • Teach those procedures to the team, schedule the work, and order and estimate materials
IIIMethods used

Sample, approve, repeat

  1. 01

    Sampling: small paint samples show the client the finish before the full surface is painted.

  2. 02

    Client review: the client approves a sample, and it becomes the standard.

  3. 03

    Procedure: the approved sample is written up step by step.

  4. 04

    Teaching: the team learns the procedure and paints at scale.

IVKey insights

What leading taught me

  1. 01

    A client decides faster when they can see a real sample than when they read a description.

  2. 02

    A finish only scales when it is written down so that every painter can repeat it.

  3. 03

    On a big team, the procedure is the design: it is what carries the approved look into every corner.

VDesign decisions

The sample is the contract

Nothing went to full scale before the client had approved a sample. The approved sample became the reference that every painter matched.

VIOutcomes

Open to guests

The attraction opened to guests at Tokyo Disneyland.

Watch the attraction on YouTube ↗

VIIReflection & next steps

Samples are prototypes

Looking back with what I learned in user-centered design, a paint sample is a prototype: it lets the client react to something real before the expensive work begins. The same habit of showing, testing and then scaling is what I bring to designing for screens.

Jurassic World Live Tour

I painted life-size animatronic dinosaurs for a touring arena show, and wrote the painting procedures that turned each faux skin into a finish a whole team could repeat.

Studio
Animax Designs, Nashville, Tennessee
Dates
July 2019 (contract) · Jan–Aug 2020
Role
Scenic artist lead
Materials
Fabric, fiberglass, silicone, latex
Techniques
Airbrush, automotive spraying, traditional painting

The work

IContext

Skin that moves

The most challenging part was making skin that is made out of fabric look like skin. Each dinosaur also combined fiberglass, silicone and latex, and every material takes paint differently, yet the whole body had to read as one living animal while it moved.

IIMy role

Scenic artist lead

  • Designed the paint processes and painted the dinosaurs
  • Painted life-size animatronic characters in a fast-paced shop
  • Painted fabric, fiberglass, silicone and latex
  • Developed painting procedures to create faux materials such as dinosaur skins
IIIMethods used

Test, then repeat

The work was hands-on: matching the finish on each material, then turning what worked into a procedure.

  • Airbrushing for fine detail and gradients
  • Automotive spraying for durable, even coats
  • Traditional painting by hand
IVKey insights

What the work taught

  1. 01

    One material, one recipe: silicone, latex, fiberglass and fabric each needed their own painting approach.

  2. 02

    A procedure is what lets a fast-paced team repeat a finish.

  3. 03

    The skin has to work in motion and from a distance, not only up close at the paint table.

VDesign decisions

Write it down

I turned the finishes into written painting procedures for faux dinosaur skins, so a look could be repeated from character to character instead of reinvented each time.

VIOutcomes

On tour

The painted characters left the shop for Jurassic World Live Tour, and the procedures stayed with the team to repeat the finishes on other characters.

See the show on tour (KREM news) ↗

VIIReflection & next steps

A process others can follow

Writing a procedure is designing for the people who use it: the next painter has to understand each step and get the same result. That is the same idea as standards and documentation in UX. Next time I would test each procedure with a painter who hadn't seen me do it, and revise the steps where they got stuck.

Sort It Out Chicago

When Chicagoans aren't sure where something goes, many throw it in the trash. Our team designed a tool that answers "where does this go?" in seconds, before they give up.

Course
HCI 440, Intro to User-Centered Design
Year
Fall 2026, 10 weeks
Team
Team Phoenix: Five by Design (5 people)
Tools
Qualtrics, Figma
Role
Personas, wireframes, design merge, heuristic review, usability observer
IContext

The trash wins by default

Chicago's residential recycling rate is low compared to other large US cities. Tools already exist and googling is easy, yet people still choose the trash when they're unsure.

Read moreClose

That told me the challenge is not only a lack of knowledge but a lack of interest in finding out. We focused on homes and buildings with up to 4 units, the buildings the City's Blue Cart service collects from.

IIMy Role (Individual Contribution)

From personas to the merged design

I co-led the personas, drew the wireframes, and merged all five teammates' wireframes into one annotated team design.

Read moreClose
  • Co-led the personas with one teammate and designed the persona pages
  • Drafted our main requirement: find the right bin fast
  • Drew the mobile and desktop wireframes for our concept report
  • Merged the team's wireframes, keeping the best ideas based on Nielsen's heuristics
  • Ran a heuristic review of my own screens and fixed what it found
  • Observed two usability sessions and wrote the observer notes
IIIResearch Methods Used

Listening at every step

  1. 12

    Semi-structured interviews (conversations guided by a few set questions): 11 residents plus 1 waste management expert. I interviewed 2 participants. Why: to hear in people's own words where sorting breaks down.

  2. 105

    Survey: eligible responses, then a second round with 72 Chicago respondents to test early design ideas. Why: to check whether the interview patterns held across many more people.

  3. 2

    Observations: watching people sort waste at a public coffee shop. Why: what people do at the bin can differ from what they say.

  4. 4

    Personas (realistic profiles that stand for groups of users), built from affinity mapping (grouping research notes by theme): Practical Sorter, Unbothered Disposer, Discouraged Neighbor, Confident Recycler. Why: to keep real people in mind at every design decision.

  5. 10

    Heuristic evaluation: checking the wireframes (simple layouts without color or images) against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics, well-known rules of thumb for easy-to-use design, and rating each issue from 0 (none) to 4 (severe). Why: to catch issues before testing with people.

  6. 5

    Moderated usability tests: watching people try real tasks on the prototype while thinking aloud, at least one participant per teammate, matched to a persona. Why: to see whether the design works for real users, and whether they learn from it.

IVKey Insights

What the research said

  1. 39%

    put an item they're unsure about in the regular trash first.

  2. 58%

    received no recycling information in the past year.

  3. 42%

    have little or no trust that their recycling gets recycled. One participant stopped recycling after hearing about a fine.

  4. 74%

    of respondents were 45 or older, so the design had to be easy to read and work without a smartphone.

  5. Obs

    Signs on bins aren't enough: food and liquid containers went into recycling despite them.

VDesign Decisions (and Why)

One question, one clear answer

In the survey, typing the item's name was the most popular way to search, and most people wanted one clear answer. So the typing search bar came first and largest, with camera and barcode as two tabs below it, each half its width.

Read moreClose
  • The answer shows right in the search suggestions, with no extra screens: every extra step is a chance to give up and use the trash.
  • Each answer shows its Chicago source and a "last reviewed" date, because trust is low.
  • QR codes on the bins, a teammate's idea I kept, so people reach the tool from where they throw things away.
  • Nothing smaller than 11 px, and darker gray text: my heuristic review rated small text severity 3, and our audience skews older.
  • No account and nothing stored per household, so residents never fear penalties for asking.
  • English and Spanish versions.
Iteration · before → after

    The team agreed the site never tells people to trash an unknown item. It shows similar items, the camera, or City guidance / 311.
  • Before

  • Considered

VIOutcomes / Evaluation

What testing showed

I observed two moderated sessions. The search worked, and it taught: after one search, a participant applied the rule on her own in the final check.

"It has one of the numbers 5, it's not black, and it has the triangle it said on the website."
Participant U1, final knowledge check

U1 and U3 are anonymous test participants. Before the test, U1 sorted plastic by habit: "It's plastic." After one search, she sorted a takeout container by the exact rule, from memory.

Read moreClose
  • Predictive search was the favorite. U3: "You could just see the answer without even selecting it." She called it "a very effective website."
  • "No exact match" recovered well. Both participants found Milk and Juice Cartons on their own.
  • Photo search gets one chance. When the photo returned the wrong item, U3 said she'd type instead and be "a lot less likely to ever use that function again." She preferred barcode.
  • Preparation steps get skipped. U3: "I might miss the blue part of how to prepare it."
  • Signs need a real question. The can character caught U1's eye, but U3 asked: "What is this gonna tell me that I don't already know?" Furniture, electronics and motor oil got her attention right away.
  • We presented the working prototype in class on Nov 17, 2026. In feedback on Milestone 3, our professor named our team as a good model for connecting evidence to design decisions.
VIIReflection & Next Steps

Underpainting first

Wireframing taught me to take a step back before deciding on the pretty factors. It's like the underpainting in a classical painting, where the structure is set in light and dark before the color goes on top.

Read moreClose

Because of my art background, I saw two needs: users needed more information, and the tool needed to add value so people would want to use it. I designed a neighborhood competition, a game with friendly characters and a point system, but the survey had no room to test them, and we delivered a tool that only searches. Next time I would spend more time learning whether users want more creative solutions, and finding the real need to address.

I also learned to get off the computer and look at my screens on a phone: on a zoomed-in monitor it's easy to miss text that people in their 40s and older can't read.

Next I would test preparation steps that are impossible to miss, a photo search that offers typing the moment it fails, and signs about hard items instead of easy ones like cans.

The Maker Lab

How do walk-in visitors get from "I have a file" to "I have a finished object" in Chicago's first free public makerspace?

Course
HCI 440, Individual Assignment 1
Date
Sept 22, 2026, one hour of open lab
Team
Solo
Methods
Structured observation, field notes, self-test, secondary research
Role
Researcher and writer
IContext

Free tools, hard steps

Anyone can walk into the Maker Lab and use 3D printers, a Cricut, laser cutters and sewing machines for free, with no sign-up. I chose it because people who can't afford machines or classes should still be able to explore their hobbies and creative needs.

IIMy Role (Individual Contribution)

Solo researcher

I planned the observation, took field notes, reviewed related research, analyzed the domain and wrote the report.

IIIResearch Methods Used

Watching without helping

  • Structured observation: 3 visitors aged about 55 to 78 and the 2 staff members helping them. I watched without interacting and described people only by role and approximate age. Why: watching shows what people actually do, not what they remember doing.
  • Field notes: a template for setting, tools, routines and key moments, with full notes written right after. Why: a template kept me looking for the same things for every visitor.
  • Self-test: I tried to follow the wall instructions and log in to a lab laptop myself. Why: to feel the first-time visitor's experience directly.
  • Secondary research: Hu, Pradhan, Bonsignore and Lazar (2024), a three-year study of a makerspace run by older adults. Why: to check whether what I saw in one hour also shows up over years elsewhere.
  • Analysis: Norman's design principles and Nielsen's heuristics (well-known rules of thumb for what makes something easy to use), plus a 5-criteria score of the challenge. Why: to name each issue with a shared vocabulary instead of my own opinion.
IVKey Insights

What I saw

  1. 39 min

    of staff time went to one Cricut sticker job, and at one point both staff members were helping the same visitor while everyone else waited.

  2. 02

    The information exists, but people can't find it: a posted sheet said files could be emailed to the printer, yet a visitor still had to ask.

  3. 03

    Instructions read like staff reminders: handwritten charts, taped printouts and laminated sheets in many formats. I couldn't follow them myself.

  4. 04

    Storage hides its contents: a visitor opened drawer after drawer to find scissors.

  5. 05

    The human help is what works: visitors valued patient one-on-one attention, something a video at home can't give.

VDesign Decisions (and Why)

Support the staff, don't replace them

This was a research study, so the output was a design opportunity: help visitors find materials and understand the steps to their own goal, while keeping the personal help they value.

  • Labels on storage that match the labels in the instructions
  • Step-by-step guides for one task at a time on complex machines like the Cricut
  • A way to check print status, like order tracking in online shopping. Nielsen calls this "visibility of system status": people should always know what's going on.
VIOutcomes / Evaluation

11 out of 15

I scored the domain 11/15: easy access to users, but too broad, since every visitor brought a different tool and need. In one hour I collected individual stories more than patterns.

VIIReflection & Next Steps

Not designing for myself

I've used makerspaces before and my art background makes me comfortable with craft materials, so I had to be careful not to design for myself. That's why I focused on older walk-in visitors instead of students like me.

Next: more visits to see which issues keep happening, and narrowing the scope to one machine, the Cricut.

About

I'm Daniela Marin, a multimedia artist from Bogotá, Colombia. I create paintings and sculptural works for homes and spaces in Colombia, made for one place and for the people who live in it.

Every commission follows design thinking: a way of creating that starts with people, not with the canvas. First I listen to your story and study your space, its light and how you move through it. Then I propose several options so you can compare and choose, and I show each piece in a rendering of your own room before I paint. Nothing is guessed, and nothing is a surprise.

It's the same process behind my scenery for Disney, Universal and National Geographic, and the one I keep sharpening in my master's studies.

She earned a BFA in Painting from the Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD), graduating with honors and winning First Place in the Senior BFA Painting Exhibition. She is now pursuing a Master of Arts in Interdisciplinary Studies at DePaul University in Chicago, with coursework in design thinking and human-centered design.

She lives and works in Chicago.

IIArtist statement

Between image and object

Daniela Marin's practice explores the porous boundary between painting and sculpture. By disrupting the traditional flatness of painting, she creates works that are neither fully object nor fully image, inviting the viewer to experience painting as something spatial, tactile and embodied.

At the heart of her work is an investigation of human relationships, memory and emotional states, approached through portraiture and abstraction. She translates personal narratives into visual metaphors rather than literal representations. She reinterprets cultural moments and subtle gestures, such as the plastic bag floating through film history, as pictorial forms that turn fleeting movement into colorful compositions of fragility and freedom. In other works, texture and form act as symbolic structures: large, opposing forms divided by negative space become a metaphor for family, where the void works like a river, showing how families flow, separate and develop their own emotional currents while staying connected.

Color is central to her portraits. Beyond realism, she uses color as an emotional language that intensifies the psychological presence, intimacy and mood of her figures. In her most recent work she has begun partially abstract portraits, letting distortion, fragmentation and material intervention reflect the instability of identity and the shifting nature of human experience.

Through material experimentation and symbolic abstraction, her work proposes a space where form, color and structure carry emotional experience, inviting viewers to project their own relationships, memories and interpretations.

IIIIn her words

Why I make art

I love creating art because it lets me translate personal and collective experiences into something visual, tangible and emotionally resonant. My practice is driven by curiosity about human relationships, memory and emotional states, things that are often hard to say in words: the emptiness when you lose someone you love, the butterflies that rise all the way to your throat when something is about to happen, or the urge to run away. I gather these sensations and turn them into art that helps me process and soothe, and that I hope resonates with others too.

I'm interested in work that exists between image and object, pushing traditional painting with three-dimensional elements that extend into space, so viewers don't just look, but feel and physically engage with it.
